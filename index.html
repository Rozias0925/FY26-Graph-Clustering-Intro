Graph Laplacian & Spectral Clustering:
Applications in Geometric Models, Urban Networks, and Medical Imaging

Exploring how graph construction and feature choices shape clustering across geometry, transportation networks, and MRI images.

Authors: Chun-Hao Wen; Hsin-Yi Ko

Advisor: Prof. Shih-Feng Hsieh

1. Introduction to Graph Laplacian

In the field of machine learning, real-world data rarely falls into simple linear or spherical distributions. K-means on raw Euclidean coordinates can struggle with non-convex clusters or representations that omit relevant relationships.

To overcome this, we introduce Graph-based methodologies. The core philosophy is: capture the intrinsic structure of the data and establish a concept of "similarity" based on that structure. Through this approach, similar data points are pulled closer together within the graph space, providing an alternative representation for exploratory clustering.

Three Pillars of Graph Construction

To construct a Graph Laplacian, we must first map our data into a graph structure using a specific perspective. This involves three critical elements:

  • Nodes: Represent independent data points (e.g., a facet on a 3D model, an MRT station, or a superpixel in a medical image).
  • Edges: Determine if there is a relationship between two nodes (e.g., spatial adjacency).
  • Weights: Quantify the "degree of similarity" between two connected nodes.

Gaussian Similarity & The Graph Laplacian

To compute weights, we utilize Gaussian Similarity. The closer the distance or the more similar the features between two nodes, the larger the computed weight:

\[ W_{i,j} = \exp\left(-\frac{||\mathbb{X}_i - \mathbb{X}_j||^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \]

Once we obtain the similarity matrix \(W\), we calculate the sum of similarities for each node to form a diagonal matrix known as the Degree Matrix (\(D\)). Finally, subtracting the similarity matrix from the degree matrix yields a symmetric, positive semi-definite Laplacian Matrix (\(L\)):

\[ L = D - W \]

This matrix encodes the similarities specified by our graph design; what it captures depends on the chosen features, edges, and weighting scheme.

2. Introduction to Spectral Clustering

The concept of Spectral Clustering is highly intuitive. It offers a graph-based spectral representation for clustering. Instead of making hard cuts in a complex, high-dimensional raw space, it utilizes the Graph Laplacian to map data into a low-dimensional feature space, which can make certain non-convex structures easier to separate.

Core Objective: Minimizing Clustering "Cost"

For an undirected graph with nonnegative weights, the Laplacian quadratic form measures how much a graph signal varies across strongly connected nodes:

\[ v^T L v = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j} w_{i,j}(v_i - v_j)^2 \]

For a connected graph, the nontrivial minimum is obtained by minimizing this energy subject to \(v^T\mathbf{1}=0\) and \(\|v\|_2=1\); the minimizer is a Fiedler eigenvector. Here, \(v_i\) and \(v_j\) are values of a graph signal, not cluster labels themselves:

  • If two data points have a very high similarity \(w_{i,j}\) but are assigned far apart during clustering (causing \((v_i - v_j)^2\) to be large), the energy increases.
  • Therefore, minimizing this cost function inherently encourages "highly similar (strongly connected) data points to be grouped into the same cluster."

Linear Algebra Solution: Physical Significance of Eigenvectors

Under these constraints, a Fiedler eigenvector solves the relaxed two-way partitioning problem for a connected graph. Larger spectral embeddings can use several small-eigenvalue eigenvectors, with conventions depending on the formulation:

  • The smallest eigenvalue \(\lambda_1 = 0\): The constant vector is a zero eigenvector of \(L=D-W\). When the graph is connected, this zero eigenvalue is simple; for a disconnected graph, its multiplicity equals the number of connected components.
  • Utilizing eigenvectors from the 2nd onward: To achieve meaningful clustering, we discard the first eigenvector and begin utilizing the eigenvector corresponding to the second smallest eigenvalue (known as the Fiedler vector). The signs and relative coordinates can suggest partitions, but their interpretation depends on graph construction and eigenvector ambiguity.

Low-Dimensional Mapping & K-means Integration

In one common unnormalized formulation, selected nontrivial eigenvectors are arranged as columns of a spectral embedding matrix; other formulations include the trivial eigenvector among the smallest \(k\) eigenvectors. The \(i\)-th row of this matrix becomes the new, low-dimensional coordinate for the \(i\)-th data point in a completely new feature space. Finally, we simply run the traditional K-means algorithm on this spectral coordinate representation to obtain a candidate partition.

3. Feature Extraction Engineering

If traditional algorithms are applied directly to raw, massive datasets (like all pixels in an image or all vertices of a 3D model), they are highly susceptible to noise, and matrix computations will explode geometrically. The breakthrough in our research lies in customized feature engineering trade-offs and optimizations. We designed exclusive feature extraction protocols for different data types:

I. Redefining Physical Units & Dimensionality Reduction

  • Superpixel Dimensionality Reduction for Medical Images (MRI): A single MRI slice easily contains tens of thousands of pixels. Treating each pixel as a node overloads matrix computations. We integrated the SLIC algorithm to aggregate similar pixels into roughly 400 "Superpixels". This reduces the graph size substantially, though superpixel boundaries and signal preservation depend on their construction.
  • 9D Feature Extraction for 3D Models (Stanford Bunny): For spatial models, we transform each triangular face into a 9-dimensional feature vector, including: Incenter Coordinates (x,y,z), PCA-projected Incenter Coordinates (x,y,z) (to represent a rotated coordinate system), and the Normal Vector (x,y,z) to describe local surface orientation. Keeping all three PCA coordinates adds no independent geometric information by itself; any benefit depends on the effective feature weighting.

II. Gradient-Free Optimization for Medical Imaging

During our practical MRI brain tumor analysis, we discovered that normal physiological structures in the brain (like sulci and ventricles) inherently possess extremely high edge contrast. If we blindly incorporate the "Gradient" features commonly used in traditional image processing, the algorithm gets severely misled by the sharp edges of normal healthy tissues.

Therefore, we made a crucial engineering decision: decisively discard gradient features. For the illustrated cases, we prioritized brightness and regional-variance features to reduce sensitivity to strong edges in normal anatomy. This choice remains dataset-specific and requires quantitative validation.

III. Feature Normalization

Because our extracted features contain varying physical meanings and numerical scales (e.g., the scale of spatial coordinates differs entirely from normal vectors), we applied strict Row Normalization to all features before they enter the Graph Laplacian. This controls the norm of each feature vector, but it does not by itself equalize the contribution of each dimension; feature scaling and weighting remain important.

Application I: Stanford Bunny 3D Geometric Clustering

To validate the algorithm's resolution capabilities in pure geometric space, we tested it on the famous 3D model introduced by Stanford University in 1994—the Stanford Bunny. The raw data we obtained was extremely rudimentary, consisting purely of point coordinates in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), encompassing over 30,000 vertices and nearly 70,000 triangular faces.

I. The Blind Spot of Traditional Euclidean Distance

Intuitively, the simplest clustering approach is to use the "incenter coordinates" of the triangular faces and apply K-means clustering using traditional Euclidean distance. However, the illustrated results show that this approach can produce less coherent geometric partitions (as seen in Figure 3), because it completely ignores the spatial geometric undulations and structural features represented by surface normal vectors.

II. Geometric Optimization of Core Parameter \(\sigma\)

As described in our feature engineering section, we shifted to a 9D feature vector. Furthermore, we adjusted the denominator parameter \(\sigma\) in the Gaussian Kernel. We calculate the "radian difference" between unit normal vectors of two faces and take the average of all radian differences. To prevent negatives or zeros, we take the maximum between this value and the computer's minimum precision "eps":

\[ \sigma = \max(\text{mean}(\text{Radian Difference}), \text{eps}) \]

Core Concept: This \(\sigma\) sets the scale of similarity decay, not a hard connectivity cutoff. Its effect on the partition depends on the features and graph sparsity.

Application II: Greater Taipei MRT Network Clustering

We applied the Graph Laplacian to urban transportation networks, innovatively utilizing "Fare Price" as the Edge distance between two stations, and assigning weights via Gaussian Similarity (with self-distance at transfer stations set to 1).

Fare as Distance: Revealing Urban Structural Shifts

Comparisons reveal that, except for the central region, the clustering results of fare and Euclidean distance are similar in several regions, suggesting fare and geographic distance encode overlapping structure in this example. More intriguingly, when we increase the cluster count from K=4 to K=6, Taipei's three main cores (Taipei Main Station, Xinyi, Neihu) are cleanly separated into independent blocks.

The originally strong links of a massive core are severed. In terms of the cost function, this means "breaking off from these sub-centers is the lower-cost choice." This partition is consistent with a polycentric interpretation, but it does not establish a historical transition or a causal urban-development mechanism. Such claims require additional mobility and land-use evidence.

Application III: Smart Segmentation of Brain Tumor MRIs

In medical imaging, having tens of thousands of pixels causes computational explosions. We introduced the SLIC Superpixels algorithm to aggregate the image into roughly 400 blocks acting as Nodes, defining Edges by "spatial adjacency."

Brightness Weights & Extreme Case Testing

We combined Gaussian weights with "Brightness": adjacent blocks with similar brightness yield higher weights, while large boundary disparities cause weights to approach zero. This naturally generates strong connections within the same physiological structures.

Feature Extraction Optimization & Improvement Efficacy

In our current upgraded phase, we officially introduced the customized feature extraction functions discussed earlier. Previously, the raw method required setting up to 7 clusters (excluding background) to separate the brain tumor from surrounding complex tissues. After deep optimization via feature engineering, the illustrative partition shows a change when shape-related features are added.

Currently, we only need to set 2 clusters (excluding background, directly bisecting into "Tumor" and "Non-Tumor") to obtain a visually clearer separation in the illustrated case. Without ground-truth masks and quantitative evaluation, neither segmentation accuracy nor clinical utility can be established.

In the illustrated feature design, circularity serves as one shape-related cue. Because brain tumors (especially malignant ones) typically exhibit irregular, infiltrative growth with rugged edges, while certain normal brain structures or noise-induced bright spots usually have smoother or elongated geometries. To give the algorithm the ability to "automatically recognize shapes," we introduced the circularity formula to quantify this feature:

\[ \text{Circularity} = \frac{4\pi \times \text{Area}}{\text{Perimeter}^2} \]

According to this geometric formula, the closer a shape is to a perfect circle, the closer its circularity is to 1. Conversely, the more elongated the shape, or the more irregular its edges (causing the perimeter in the denominator to spike), the circularity value drops sharply toward 0. We combined Brightness and Circularity at a 4:6 ratio, enabling the algorithm to not only see "light and dark" but also "feel shapes." This may help distinguish regions with different shapes in these illustrations, but it cannot reliably distinguish tumor from normal tissue without broader evaluation.

In the future, we plan to expand from 2D to 3D imaging, incorporating more textural features or lightweight machine learning models, allowing this Graph Laplacian-based segmentation technology to be evaluated for potential clinical research use after quantitative validation.

Scope and Limitations

These are exploratory examples, not benchmarked demonstrations of universal superiority. Partitions depend on features, similarity scales, and clustering settings. The MRI illustrations lack ground-truth-based segmentation metrics and do not constitute a clinical diagnostic system; MRT groupings do not demonstrate urban causality.
